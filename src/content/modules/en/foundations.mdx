## The first-day trap

An interoperability project nearly always starts the same way: two teams meet, agree on a format,
open a flow, watch the message arrive — and declare success.

Six months later, nobody uses the data. It arrives, it is valid, and it is useless. This module
exists to spare you that outcome, by giving you the vocabulary to say **where exactly** an exchange
fails.

## Why health interoperability is hard

Healthcare is not a complicated technical domain. It is a **complex socio-technical** one: data
there cannot be separated from care processes and from responsibilities.

A technically perfect exchange between two systems that do not share a business need produces no
value. **The hard part is not transport — it is alignment.**
</Callout>

## Three words that must not be confused

The European framework carefully separates three notions. Mixing them up is the most common source
of misunderstanding between a clinical directorate and an IT department.

The definition adopted by the European framework is explicit about this:

> Interoperability is the ability of disparate and diverse organisations to interact towards mutually
> beneficial and agreed common goals, involving the sharing of information and knowledge between the
> organisations, through the business processes they support, by means of the exchange of data
> between their respective ICT systems.

Read it backwards: data exchange comes **last**. It is a means, not the goal.

## The six ReEIF layers

The *Refined eHealth European Interoperability Framework* (ReEIF) was adopted by the eHealth Network
in November 2015. It takes the generic four-level European framework and splits two of its levels:

- the **organisational** level becomes *Policy / governance* and *Care process*, because those two
  planes involve different actors and different responsibilities;
- the **technical** level becomes *Applications* (health-specific technology) and *IT infrastructure*
  (generic technology), because they obey different classes of standards.

The result: six layers. Open them in whatever order appeals to you — but read all six.

<Callout variant="warning">
A perfectly valid FHIR resource can still miss the interoperability goal if the other layers are
absent. **Capability at one layer does not prove full interoperability.**
</Callout>

## Worked example — publishing a document to a national record

Take a mundane act: an organisation publishes a discharge report into the patient's shared record.
Walk down the six layers, question by question.

<Reveal question="The national service is reachable securely. Which layers does that prove?">
Only **infrastructure**, and partly **applications**: you know the pipe exists and that the system
can package a document. You know nothing about the meaning of its content, its clinical use, or who
is accountable for corrections.
</Reveal>

<Reveal question="The document arrives and displays correctly. Is the project finished?">
No. Three questions remain open:

- **Information** — does the receiver understand every coded element, or is it reading local codes
  that mean nothing on its side?
- **Care process** — who uses this document, at what point in the pathway, to decide what?
- **Policy and legal** — who is accountable for publication, correction and use? Is the exchange
  lawful *in this precise context*?
</Reveal>

## From use case to implementation

ReEIF is not only a diagnostic grid. It is also a work order. The constant temptation is to start
from the technical specification; the effective order is the opposite.

<StepFlow
  caption="The specification is not the starting point. The care outcome is."
  steps={[
    {
      title: 'Expected care outcome',
      body: 'What must patients and professionals achieve? Which decision becomes possible?',
    },
    {
      title: 'Information content',
      body: 'Which data, concepts, codes and units are required?',
    },
    {
      title: 'Application exchange',
      body: 'Which document, message, API, profile or transaction carries that content?',
    },
    {
      title: 'Technical path',
      body: 'How does the information travel safely and reliably?',
    },
    {
      title: 'Verification',
      body: 'Which agreements, responsibilities and regulatory constraints apply?',
    },
  ]}
/>

<Callout variant="reflect">
Think of a flow you know. Without opening any documentation: could you name **one** decision taken
at each of the six layers? The one you cannot find is probably the one that will hurt.
</Callout>

## Takeaway

Health interoperability is multidimensional. Technical exchange is **necessary but not sufficient**.
ReEIF solves nothing on its own: it gives you a map of the rest of the problem, and a shared language
for discussing it with people whose job is not yours.
