## The problem: vital data, scattered everywhere

In a hospital, value is never inside one isolated application. It appears the moment data can be
**linked**:

- clinical data, for care;
- administrative data, for organisation;
- logistics data, for operations;
- secondary data, for research and management.

To standardise is to make that data reusable **without rebuilding a bespoke interface for every new
use**. That is the only difference that matters.

Hospitals long chose **homogenisation** over standardisation: local simplification instead of global
openness. The result: uniform deployments, a proliferation of bespoke connectors, and still no
interoperability.
</Callout>

## Without standards, software grows and silos remain

Watch the cycle. It replays identically in every organisation.

Vendors respond rationally: lacking modular interoperability, they widen their functional footprint.
Software becomes sprawling monoliths, hard to evolve, and each new module demands its own interfaces.
The organisation loses the ability to adopt innovative solutions.

<Callout variant="key">
Standardising = turning a **one-off integration** into **reusable infrastructure**.
</Callout>

## Automation does not exist without reliable data

Automating means turning data into actions: alarms, reminders, monitoring, equipment
synchronisation. A reliable automatic action therefore requires three properties at once.

weakest in your context today? That is the one that will break first.
</Callout>

## Case study — Saint Joseph Hospital, and the birth of Argus

A mid-sized hospital: visible scope, real complexity. The essential flows are known — identity,
stays, schedules, staff, structures, equipment. The business need is clear: supervision,
traceability, understanding of flows.

The teaching goal of the case is to see how the **progressive destruction of silos** lets an
observation and steering platform emerge: **Argus**, a hospital control tower.

### From heterogeneous flows to an off-board platform

The EHR and administrative systems supply identities and stays; the scheduling system supplies
appointments, resources and availability; HR supplies staff, roles and assignments; reference
registries supply structures, places and equipment. Everything converges into an **off-board FHIR
platform**, then into Argus — a supervision layer built on standardised, traceable, reusable data.

<Callout variant="warning">
Technical conversion is not enough. You must stabilise the **business objects**, their
**provenance** and their **lifecycle**. Converting HL7 v2 to FHIR without that produces unreadable
JSON instead of unreadable pipes.
</Callout>

<Reveal question="At what point have you genuinely standardised, rather than merely connected?">
When a **second** use case can consume the same business object without anyone writing a new
interface. As long as each new need reopens a mapping project, you have connected — you have not
standardised.
</Reveal>

## Takeaway

We do not standardise to be tidy. We standardise to make the hospital **observable**, **governable**
and **automatable**.
