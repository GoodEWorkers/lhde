## The anti-pattern: the spaghetti information system

EHR, laboratory, imaging, scheduling, HR, billing, beds, equipment, theatre, management. Between each
of them, a bespoke interface. Every link carries its own logic, its own mapping, its own security and
recovery rules.

The consequences are always the same: technical debt, inconsistent data, difficult traceability,
fragmented security, every project starting over, and almost no reuse.

<Callout variant="key">
The problem is not "getting a message through". It is designing an architecture that can **last**.
</Callout>

### The terminology server: the backbone of meaning

Without a terminology service, semantics stays **hard-coded inside applications** — duplicated,
divergent and impossible to fix globally. The five operations from module 4 (`$expand`, `$lookup`,
`$validate-code`, `$translate`, `$subsumes`) become an architectural component here.

<Callout variant="key">
Architectural rule: applications **consume** meaning, they do not reinvent it.
</Callout>

### Reference registries: the foundations everyone forgets

Patient, professionals, organisation, place, equipment, appointments. If the registries are unstable,
the architecture is unstable — regardless of how good the FHIR server on top is.

<Callout variant="reflect">
In your context, which of those six registries is least governed? That one sets the ceiling for every
interoperability project to come.
</Callout>

## Traceability: making data explainable

Interoperable data must be understandable in its **context of production and transformation**.

**Source system → transformation → FHIR resource → use.** At every step, six elements must remain
attachable: source of truth, date and version, producing author or system, applied transformation
rule, consent and legal basis, access and consultations.

<Callout variant="key">
Traceability = trust + auditability + **the ability to correct**. The third term is the one people
forget.
</Callout>

## Rights management: interoperability must stay controlled

The more data circulates, the more rights must be explicit, centralised and auditable. The typical
path: *identity provider (IAM / SSO) → API gateway (policy enforcement) → FHIR and data services →
applications and users.*

## Architecture governance: who decides what?

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Data owner** | Defines business meaning, expected quality, usage rules. |
| **Product / application owner** | Exposes or consumes APIs under the exchange contract. |
| **Interop team** | Maintains profiles, mappings, validators and internal standards. |
| **Security / DPO** | Defines access, consent, audit and compliance. |
| **Operations** | Monitors flows, errors, performance and incidents. |

<Callout variant="warning">
A good architecture model above all stops **every project inventing its own rules**. That, not the
choice of server, is where durability is decided.
</Callout>

## Argus target architecture

The sources — EHR, scheduling, HR, maintenance, bed management, laboratory — flow through connectors
and mappings into an **interoperability platform** exposing FHIR APIs, terminology, IAM, audit and
validation. The uses — management, alerting, research — then plug into **normalised data**.

<Callout variant="key">
Principle: uses plug into **governed services**, not directly into each silo.
</Callout>

<Reveal question="How do you feed a unified picture without recreating a monolith?">
By separating three things projects routinely merge:

1. **Capture** — connectors and mappings, specific by nature and accepted as such.
2. **Foundation** — stabilised business objects, governed, versioned, exposed through shared services.
3. **Use** — applications that consume the foundation without the right to rewrite its rules.

The monolith reappears the moment a use case imposes its needs directly on a connector.
</Reveal>

## Takeaway

Durable interoperability is an **architectural property**, not just a format one.

- A good system exposes governed services rather than an accumulation of connectors.
- The core services — identity, terminology, rights, audit, validation, transformation — must be
  designed from the start.
- Traceability and rights management are not layers added afterwards.
- Value appears when data can be combined, understood, secured and **used to act**.
